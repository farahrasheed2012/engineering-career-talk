ICLT Sunday School

Engineering?

Is it for you? What even is it? And what can you try while you’re still in high school?

So… what is it?

Engineers are people who make stuff that actually works.

  • You spot a problem → you build a fix → you test it → you make it better.
  • It’s not just “being good at math.” It’s being curious and stubborn in a good way.
  • Your phone, games, cars, hospitals, bridges — someone engineered those.

My path

I work in computer science & computer engineering territory.

  • Computer Science (CS) — more about software, algorithms, apps, data, AI.
  • Think: “How do we make the computer solve this problem?”
  • College majors often called CS, Software Engineering, or similar.
  • Computer Engineering (CE) — software + the hardware it runs on.
  • Think: chips, devices, embedded systems — code that talks to real electronics.
  • Sits between electrical engineering and CS.

Lots of jobs blend both — that’s the mix I live in day to day.

A normal day

What I actually do all day.

  • Check in with my team — what’s broken, what’s shipping next, who needs unblock.
  • Write and review code — often software that runs close to the hardware.
  • Hunt bugs. Search. Ask. Try again. Help others do the same.
  • Test changes, then ship so real products (and real people) get the fix.

Not movie hacking. More: build → break → fix → ship → repeat.

Why bother?

Because the cool stuff you use didn’t appear by magic.

  • Clean water, safer cars, medical tools, reliable phones — engineers helped make those real.
  • Skill is a way to serve: family, neighbors, mosque, school, people you’ll never meet.
  • And yes — it’s also a real career with lots of paths. Service and a job can be the same work.

Kinds of engineers · 1 of 2

There’s not just one kind.

Mechanical Design things that move — robots, cars, bikes, machines. If you like building with your hands + physics, this is a classic path.
Electrical Circuits, chips, power, headphones, phone guts. Think: “how does electricity become something useful?”
Civil Roads, bridges, buildings, water systems. The stuff you notice when it works… and really notice when it doesn’t.
Chemical Turn materials into useful stuff — fuels, plastics, medicines. Lab work plus big real-world processes.

Kinds of engineers · 2 of 2

A few more doors.

Biomedical Engineering meets health — imaging, implants, hospital tools, robotic surgery systems. Great if you like biology and building things that help people.
Aerospace Planes, satellites, rockets, drones. Same physics energy as mechanical — just aimed at the sky.
Industrial Make systems faster and less wasteful — factories, logistics, workflows. Puzzle people who like optimizing “how does this whole thing run?”
Software Code behind apps, games, websites, and company tools. We’ll zoom into this later — it’s my day job.

Plot twist

These fields mix more than the labels suggest.

  • A robot? Mechanical + electrical + software hanging out together.
  • A hospital device or surgical robot? Biomedical + mechanical + software people on the same team.
  • You don’t have to “pick forever” in 9th grade. Try stuff. Notice what you like.
  • Narrow later — after you’ve poked a few doors.

In high school

What actually helps — classes + habits.

  • Math — algebra → geometry, precalc, calculus if you can.
  • Physics — how the physical world behaves.
  • Chemistry — especially for chem / materials / biomedical.
  • CS / coding — useful everywhere; must-have for software.
  • English / writing — you’ll explain ideas to humans.
  • Curiosity — “why did that break?” is a superpower.
  • Not quitting — stuck is normal; stuck forever is optional.
  • Teamwork — almost nothing ships alone.
  • You don’t need to be a genius. You need to keep showing up.

How we win

Same loop real engineers use.

  • 1–2 min: sketch a plan (base matters more than height fantasies).
  • Build. If it falls — good. That’s data.
  • Rebuild smarter. Folds, tubes, and a wide base beat a wobbly card stack.
  • Done when time’s up. We measure. We clap. We laugh at the dramatic collapses.

The path (simplified)

High school → college → job. That’s the usual movie — usual, not only.

  • Now: classes + clubs + anything you build for fun.
  • College: engineering major, computer science, or something close — common path, not the only door.
  • In between: internships, projects, people who’ll vouch for you.
  • After: tons of places to work — companies, startups, research, you name it.

Quick share · a few volunteers

Okay, AI

AI is everywhere — and engineers build a lot of it.

  • Chatbots, photo filters, recommendations, coding helpers — people engineered those systems.
  • It’s fast. It’s also sometimes wrong with total confidence.
  • Use it like a coach sitting next to you — not like a robot doing your brain for you.
  • You’ll probably work with it someday either way. Knowing how stuff works beats being scared of it.

But wait

If AI can spit out answers… why still learn math and science?

  • Math trains you to spot patterns and catch nonsense numbers.
  • Science trains you to test ideas instead of guessing.
  • Tech literacy means you know what a tool can and can’t do.
  • Your own judgment is the filter — “does this answer even make sense?”

Getting an answer is easy now. Knowing if it’s any good? That’s on you.

Looking ahead

Jobs will keep changing. That’s normal.

  • AI as a teammate — less “type every line by hand,” more “steer the tool and check the result.”
  • Mixed teams — software people, hardware people, designers, business folks in the same chat.
  • Judgment still wins — safety, cost, ethics, “should we ship this?”
  • Keep learning — what you use at 30 won’t be only what you learned at 18. That’s okay.

Bigger than a résumé

Engineering is a way to help people.

  • Build for others — safer tools, clearer apps, cleaner water, better classrooms, care for elders.
  • Start close to home — mosque, school, neighborhood, family. Who has a problem you could ease?
  • Learn so you can give — hard classes and projects aren’t just for applications; they grow what you can offer.
  • Show up with people — sports, arts, tutoring, volunteering. Character travels with your skills.

Since you’ll ask anyway

Three honest answers.

  • Money: many engineering jobs pay well, but it varies a lot by role and city — don’t pick a path only for the paycheck.
  • Job outlook: good overall in a lot of engineering areas, but hot titles change; learning how to learn beats chasing one buzzword.
  • Non-college routes: real options exist (trades, certifications, military tech, apprenticeships) — college is common for engineering, not the only door into tech.
